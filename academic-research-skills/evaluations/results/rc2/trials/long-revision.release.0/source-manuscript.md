# A perhaps limited exploration of average comparisons across two evaluation conditions

Synthetic Skill-development manuscript. This is an editable test document, not a research publication.

## Abstract
We have attempted to examine a potentially interesting comparison between procedures A and B.
The possibility that evaluation composition might matter is perhaps not entirely surprising, but it may
still be useful to describe what can currently be said. In C1, the target-weighted loss was 7.40 for A
and 6.72 for B, giving an A−B difference of 0.68 loss points. The older C2 comparison gave losses
6.74 and 7.61 and a difference of −0.87 points. A fixed-C1-weight comparison in C2 gave −0.15.
These quantities might suggest that a favorable average comparison need not hold uniformly.
We cannot claim a universal result and do not have all possible data. Our work was exploratory and
we believe that additional work would be needed to determine broader implications.

## Introduction
Researchers commonly compare procedures through average performance. An average can summarize many
outcomes while making it difficult to see whether the same judgment applies to the groups included in
the comparison. When evaluation conditions change, both the relative performance within a group and
the population represented by the average may change. These are different questions, even if a single
summary number changes sign. In this fixture we ask what the supplied paired comparisons establish
and how a change of target composition affects the reported average.

It may be prudent to mention that this question is only being explored in a small demonstration.
The study does not establish a new general method and is not intended to guarantee that either
procedure will succeed in a new environment. We nevertheless assembled a comparison using the
available records and several useful calculation steps. The current materials may be sufficient for
some observations but are not sufficient for every conclusion that might be drawn.

The available N1 summary describes a favorable average under balanced composition. We did not obtain
its full methods or data, so we cannot determine whether its losses, procedures and evaluation units
are directly comparable with this fixture. N2 distinguishes target weights from observed sample
proportions. N3 states that tuning preceded this demonstration. These notes supply different kinds
of evidence: an external-looking summary cannot be treated as an independent validation of our
current comparison simply because it also reports an average advantage.

Our intended contribution is a transparent account of a conditional average comparison. In doing so,
we hope to show where that comparison is informative and where group-level counterexamples change
its interpretation. The purpose is not to select a convenient favorable subgroup, to alter the loss
metric after examining the results, or to claim a causal explanation for the change between C1 and C2.
We retain the original groups and the target populations specified in the notes.

## Materials and comparison
The materials are the six synthetic rows in the frozen table and four local evidence notes. There are
30 independent clusters in C1: 16 in G1, 4 in G2 and 10 in G3. There are also 30 in C2: 6, 15 and 9.
Procedures A and B were evaluated on the same clusters within each condition and group. Clusters
are independent between the conditions. A row is a summary for a group; six rows are not six
independent individual observations suitable for a generic six-point significance test.

Loss is expressed in loss points, with smaller values preferred. For C1 the target weights are 0.5,
0.2 and 0.3; for C2 they are 0.2, 0.5 and 0.3. They describe the target comparison, rather than the
observed fraction of clusters. We report both procedures' target-weighted means and their difference.
For the sensitivity calculation, we apply the C1 target weights to C2 group means while retaining
C2 outcomes. This calculation holds the composition fixed for description; it is not a causal
intervention and cannot assign the observed difference to a mechanism.

There were several preparatory operations before we wrote the present text. We inspected the table,
read the notes and made a list of comparisons. We then calculated weighted averages, examined the
group-level directions and considered whether the table might be helpful for making a figure.
Those operations helped prepare the document. They do not by themselves count as independent
evidence about either procedure's performance, although we may have initially described them in
some detail to make our work seem transparent.

## Uncertainty and research status
The supplied marginal intervals for A and B and paired-difference intervals are 95% percentile
cluster-bootstrap intervals. They summarize uncertainty within a group and condition. The paired
differences incorporate the paired evaluation at that level; interval overlap between A and B does
not determine whether the interval for A−B contains zero. There are no cluster observations,
aggregate bootstrap draws or cross-group covariances in the supplied materials.

N3 records that the demonstration follows tuning. We therefore describe the comparisons as
exploratory. There is no preregistration, randomized assignment, equivalence margin or external
evaluation supplied. We should perhaps repeatedly note that none of the results establishes a
universal guarantee. Such qualifications may be needed when interpreting an average as a broadly
applicable recommendation, but the arithmetic itself remains reproducible from the table.

## Results: C1
In G1, A had loss 10 and B had loss 8. The paired improvement A−B was 2, with a supplied interval
from 1 to 3. In G2, the corresponding losses were 6 and 7, with difference −1 and interval −1.8
to −0.2. In G3, the losses were 4 and 4.4, giving −0.4 with interval −0.7 to −0.1. Thus B had a
favorable comparison in G1 and an unfavorable comparison in G2 and G3.

With the C1 target weights, the average loss was 7.40 for A and 6.72 for B. The difference 0.68
is positive in the direction favoring B. A favorable target average therefore coexists with two
groups in which B has higher loss. This is a concrete limit on an interpretation that every group
benefits. It does not make the target-weighted comparison mathematically incorrect, nor does it
establish that the target weighting is appropriate for every possible decision.

## Results: C2 and fixed composition
In C2, G1 losses were 9 and 8.4, with difference 0.6 and interval 0.1 to 1.1. G2 losses were 7
and 8.8, giving −1.8 with interval −2.5 to −1.1. The earlier G3 value for B was 5.1 against 4.8
for A, producing difference −0.3. The old aggregate used the current C2 weights to report 6.74
for A and 7.61 for B and a difference of −0.87 points.

Applying the C1 weights to those older C2 values gave means 7.34 and 7.49, with difference −0.15.
The comparison therefore remained unfavorable to B even under the earlier target composition.
This calculation may be useful, but it does not show how much of the change was caused by
composition. It uses fixed summaries and supplies neither a causal intervention nor an estimate
of a cross-condition statistical interaction.

## Discussion
The results may provide a useful demonstration of the distinction between a target average and a
group-wide claim. In C1 the target average favors B, while G2 and G3 do not. In C2 the target
average favors A. Keeping the C1 composition for the C2 summaries leaves the comparison unfavorable
to B. These results establish a conditional ordering across the supplied comparisons. They do not
establish that B is uniformly better, uniformly worse, or unsuitable for every deployment.

There are at least two descriptions of the between-condition change: the distribution represented by
the target weights changes, and within-group performance changes. The fixed-weight calculation
helps readers separate descriptive questions. It does not uniquely identify an explanatory mechanism.
We cannot obtain the unreported cluster-level structure by treating bootstrap intervals as raw data,
and we cannot infer the significance of the aggregate reversal without its joint uncertainty.

The N1 summary might motivate a question about comparison conditions, but we have not read its
full methods or established that it represents a separate cohort. Agreement with that summary
would not automatically provide replication, and disagreement would not necessarily establish a
scientific contradiction. The currently supported contribution is the explicit interpretation of
these frozen comparisons. A later external evaluation should define the target population, retain
group-level counterexamples and use appropriate independent units.

## Limitations
The source is a synthetic fixture with summary data. No independent external research was conducted.
The group-wise intervals are available, while aggregate uncertainty and unit-level records are not.
The comparison follows tuning and has no prospective registration. These conditions limit
inference and transfer. They do not prevent checking the arithmetic or recognizing that an
average ordering and a uniform group-level ordering differ.

It might also be helpful to acknowledge these same limitations again because the reader could
otherwise infer that this document provides a universal conclusion. We have not performed every
possible alternative analysis, and we cannot claim that all future conditions would resemble these
ones. We may need to be careful about wording, perhaps especially when explaining the apparent
advantages, since the observations are only a limited illustration.

## Conclusion
A positive C1 average difference of 0.68 coexists with unfavorable G2 and G3 comparisons. The older
C2 average difference was −0.87, and the older fixed-C1-weight difference was −0.15. The direction
of the average comparison depends on the supplied evaluation condition and target composition.
The fixture supports reporting the target and group-level comparisons together. It does not
establish causal attribution, aggregate significance, equivalence or general deployment benefit.
